Abstract. For an infinite ordinal α\alpha let Ξ1(α)\Xi_1(\alpha) be the supremum of the ordinals D(α)D(\alpha) over all recursive dilators DD; it is the least ordinal above α\alpha that is closed under every recursive dilator. Let Ξ1′(α)\Xi'_1(\alpha) be the same supremum over all recursive predilators that are well-ordered at α\alpha. The notation Ξ1\Xi_1 is Girard’s; Ressayre (1982) writes PAD\mathrm{PAD} and PAD′\mathrm{PAD}'. Ξ1′(α)\Xi'_1(\alpha) is also the supremum of the well-orderings that are Σ1\Sigma_1-definable over LαL_{\alpha} without parameters. Ressayre showed that Ξ1(α)<Ξ1′(α)\Xi_1(\alpha) < \Xi'_1(\alpha) can happen. We determine where it first happens. Let uu be the least ordinal that is uncountable in the next admissible set Lu+L_{u^+}, and let μ0\mu_0 be the least ordinal that is not Σ1\Sigma_1-definable from uu in Lu+L_{u^+}. Then Ξ1′(d)=Ξ1(d)\Xi'_1(d) = \Xi_1(d) for every infinite d<μ0d < \mu_0, while Ξ1(x)<Ξ1′(x)=Ξ1(Ξ1(x))<μ0+\Xi_1(x) < \Xi'_1(x) = \Xi_1(\Xi_1(x)) < \mu_0^+ for every xx with μ0≤x<Ξ1(μ0)\mu_0 \le x < \Xi_1(\mu_0). We give a criterion for Ξ1′(α)=Ξ1(α)\Xi'_1(\alpha) = \Xi_1(\alpha) at an arbitrary α\alpha, and characterise μ0\mu_0 as the least ordinal that shares all its Σ11\Sigma^1_1 properties with a larger ordinal. As an application we show that a Σ11\Sigma^1_1 condition on a family of partial operations makes the least ordinal closed under the family equal to Ξ1(α)\Xi_1(\alpha), and we record, partly from cited results, where the next admissible ordinal, the supremum δα\delta_{\alpha} of the well-orderings Δ1\Delta_1-definable over LαL_{\alpha} with parameters, and the parameter-free Σ1\Sigma_1 form stop being equal to Ξ1(α)\Xi_1(\alpha).

1. Introduction

Girard’s dilators are the functors on the category of linear orders that preserve direct limits and pullbacks and send well-orders to well-orders; a predilator need not send well-orders to well-orders (Section 2.1). For an infinite ordinal α\alpha put Ξ1(α)=sup⁡{|D(α)|:D a recursive dilator},Ξ1′(α)=sup⁡{|P(α)|:P a recursive predilator, P(α) well-ordered},\begin{align*} \Xi_1(\alpha) &= \sup\{|D(\alpha)| : D \text{ a recursive dilator}\}, \\ \Xi'_1(\alpha) &= \sup\{|P(\alpha)| : P \text{ a recursive predilator, } P(\alpha) \text{ well-ordered}\}, \end{align*} where |W||W| is the order type of a well-order WW. The notation is Girard’s: in Volume 2 of his book (Corollary 11.B.7) Ξ1\Xi_1 is the sum of all recursive dilators, whose value at α\alpha is Ξ1(α)\Xi_1(\alpha), and Ξ1′\Xi'_1 follows his Exercise 11.B.12. Ressayre (1982) writes PAD\mathrm{PAD} and PAD′\mathrm{PAD}'. He defines the two ordinals with a slightly different class of functors, the primitive recursive functors on the finite ordinals extended by direct limits; we write PADℛ(α)\mathrm{PAD}_{\mathcal R}(\alpha) and PADℛ′(α)\mathrm{PAD}'_{\mathcal R}(\alpha) for his ordinals, and Corollary 5 shows that PADℛ(α)=Ξ1(α)\mathrm{PAD}_{\mathcal R}(\alpha) = \Xi_1(\alpha) and PADℛ′(α)=Ξ1′(α)\mathrm{PAD}'_{\mathcal R}(\alpha) = \Xi'_1(\alpha) for infinite α\alpha. At a finite argument nn both of Ressayre’s ordinals are ω\omega, because his functors have finite values at nn and the constant functors are among them; so only infinite arguments are considered.

Throughout, α+\alpha^+ is the least admissible ordinal above α\alpha. Girard proved that Ξ1(α)=α+\Xi_1(\alpha) = \alpha^+ for many ordinals α\alpha, among them every infinite α\alpha below the ordinal σ\sigma defined in Section 1.1 (Girard’s book, Volume 2, Theorem 11.B.6 and Corollary 11.B.7). Ressayre gave a new proof and asked how Ξ1(α)\Xi_1(\alpha), Ξ1′(α)\Xi'_1(\alpha) and α+\alpha^+ can be related. He showed Ξ1(α)≤Ξ1′(α)≤α+\Xi_1(\alpha) \le \Xi'_1(\alpha) \le \alpha^+, proved equality of the first two in several cases, and gave the first example of inequality (his Theorem IV.7): if λ\lambda is the least ordinal that is not countable in Lλ+L_{\lambda^+}, and μ\mu is the least ordinal above λ\lambda that is not Σ1\Sigma_1-definable from λ\lambda in Lλ+L_{\lambda^+}, then PADℛ(λ)=PADℛ(μ)<PADℛ′(μ)<μ+.\mathrm{PAD}_{\mathcal R}(\lambda) = \mathrm{PAD}_{\mathcal R}(\mu) < \mathrm{PAD}'_{\mathcal R}(\mu) < \mu^+ . In the proof he writes μ=λ+p0\mu = \lambda + p_0, where p0<λp_0 < \lambda is the least ordinal that is not Σ1\Sigma_1-definable from λ\lambda in Lλ+L_{\lambda^+}; he does not evaluate the two functions at p0p_0.

This paper determines the least ordinal at which the two functions differ. It is p0p_0, which we call μ0\mu_0.

1.2 The next closure point

Girard’s theorem identifies the next admissible ordinal α+\alpha^+ with the least closure point of the recursive dilators above α\alpha, for α<σ\alpha < \sigma. Ordinal collapsing functions are built over a sequence of such “next” ordinals, each closed under the operations available below it, and the question behind this paper is which function of α\alpha gives the least closure point above α\alpha in general. Besides Ξ1(α)\Xi_1(\alpha) itself, three functions are candidates: α+\alpha^+, the ordinal δα\delta_{\alpha} modelled on Aguilera’s δα\delta_{\alpha}, and its parameter-free form δα0=Ξ1′(α)\delta^0_{\alpha} = \Xi'_1(\alpha). This paper does not define a collapsing function. It records where each candidate stops being the least closure point (table below), and first gives a result that explains the outcome.

1.3 Overview of the proofs

The criterion. A Σ11\Sigma^1_1 condition on a well-order bb is the existence of an infinite branch through a tree whose nodes carry finitely many labels from bb and whose membership test reads only the order pattern of the labels. The nodes of such a tree, in the Kleene–Brouwer order (Section 3.1), form a recursive predilator in bb (Lemma 3.1). If the nodes also follow a relation ⊲\triangleleft that the guessed structure defines, the rank of the tree is at least the height of ⊲\triangleleft, that is, its rank as a well-founded relation (Lemma 3.2). For Theorem 2 (a), right to left, the guessed structure is a witness for β∈X\beta \in X and ⊲\triangleleft is the order of F(β)F(\beta): the tree is well-founded at every ordinal because FF is well-ordered on XX, so it gives a recursive dilator, and its value at α\alpha exceeds |F(α)||F(\alpha)|. This is the argument of Ressayre’s Theorem IV.6, which he credits in its essential step to Harrington, with a weaker hypothesis and with trees in place of models of set theory. Left to right is immediate: if a dilator DD bounds FF at α\alpha, the class of β\beta at which F(β)F(\beta) embeds in D(β)D(\beta) is Σ11\Sigma^1_1.

Failure at μ0\mu_0. The collapse of HH sends uu to μ0\mu_0 and every Σ1\Sigma_1 fact about uu in Lu+L_{u^+} to a true fact about μ0\mu_0. Since Σ11\Sigma^1_1 properties of a countable ordinal β\beta are Π1\Pi_1 properties of β\beta in Lβ+L_{\beta^+}, every Σ11\Sigma^1_1 class containing μ0\mu_0 contains uu (Lemma 8.3). So by Theorem 2 (a) a recursive predilator F0F_0 that first fails at uu has |F0(μ0)|≥Ξ1(μ0)|F_0(\mu_0)| \ge \Xi_1(\mu_0).

Agreement below μ0\mu_0. Let d<μ0d < \mu_0 and let rr be the least β−\beta^--definable ordinal above dd; then r≤ur \le u. The set ZdZ_d of indices of recursive predilators whose value at dd is a well-order of type below Ξ1(d)\Xi_1(d) contains every index of a recursive dilator, and it is Σ1\Sigma_1-definable from rr in Lr+L_{r^+}. A self-referential sentence then produces an index in ZdZ_d of a predilator that is well-ordered exactly below rr (Lemma 7.3). By Theorem 2 (a) that predilator is well-ordered on a Σ11\Sigma^1_1 class containing dd, which therefore lies below rr, and Theorem 2 (b) gives equality. The lemma needs rr to be β\beta-definable; for r<ur < u this is Lemma 7.1, and for r=ur = u it is a remark of Ressayre (Lemma 7.2).

The values. Ξ1(μ0)=γ\Xi_1(\mu_0) = \gamma because the isomorphisms between the orders D(u)D(u) and their order types lie in HH and collapse to isomorphisms for D(μ0)D(\mu_0) (Lemma 8.4). 1.4 Relation to the literature

Ressayre (1982). The ordinals uu and μ0\mu_0 are defined there; he shows that uu is β\beta-definable, that μ0<u\mu_0 < u, and Theorem IV.7 as quoted above (Remark IV.5, Theorem IV.7). He also shows that the two functions agree at every β\beta-definable ordinal, at every ordinal that is the last element of a Σ11\Sigma^1_1 class, and at every ordinal above the least stable ordinal (the least α\alpha such that LαL_{\alpha} is a Σ1\Sigma_1-elementary substructure of LL), and that they differ cofinally often below it (IV.3, IV.6, IV.8). We did not find in that paper the values of the two functions at p0p_0, the question of the least ordinal at which they differ, Theorem 2 (b) as a criterion, or Theorem 3. Theorem 2 (a), right to left, is the proof of his IV.6 with the hypothesis “α\alpha is the last element of a Σ11\Sigma^1_1 class” replaced by “FF is well-ordered on a Σ11\Sigma^1_1 class containing α\alpha”. The self-referential sentence in Lemma 7.3 follows the method of his proof of Girard’s theorem, which he attributes to Harrington.

Other sources. Gostanian, and Aguilera in a sharper form, determine when an admissible α\alpha is Gandy, that is, when Aguilera’s δα\delta_{\alpha} equals α+\alpha^+; we do not use their results. Gostanian calls such α\alpha good. The name Gandy, introduced by Abramson and Sacks (1976), refers to that theorem. Girard calls the ordinals of Lemma 3.4 weakly β\beta-definable (Remark 11.B.5). We found no comparison of the two functions above σ\sigma in Annexes 11.B and 11.C. We have not read Girard and Ressayre (1985), the rest of Girard’s book, or the later literature on dilators with this question in mind. Novelty of Theorems 1, 3 and 4 is not established.

2. Preliminaries

Ordinals are denoted by α,β,η,ξ,ρ,ζ,κ\alpha, \beta, \eta, \xi, \rho, \zeta, \kappa; dd, xx and yy are also used for ordinals where stated. In Ressayre’s terms “β\beta-definable” and “β−\beta^--definable” the letter β\beta is part of the name and is not a variable. Countable means finite or countably infinite.

2.1 Recursive predilators and dilators

We use Girard’s normal form (Girard 1981, Theorem 2.3.12 and Remarks 2.3.13). The order pattern of two finite tuples s,s′s, s' from a linear order is the total preorder that the order induces on their positions; for given lengths there are finitely many order patterns. A recursive predilator PP is given by a recursive set Tr⊆ω\mathrm{Tr} \subseteq \omega of traces, a recursive function ar:Tr→ω\mathrm{ar} : \mathrm{Tr} \to \omega, the arity, and a recursive comparison, which assigns one of <<, ==, >> to two traces z,z′z, z' together with an order pattern of an increasing ar(z)\mathrm{ar}(z)-tuple and an increasing ar(z′)\mathrm{ar}(z')-tuple. For a linear order bb, P(b)P(b) is the set of pairs (z,s)(z, s) with z∈Trz \in \mathrm{Tr} and ss an increasing ar(z)\mathrm{ar}(z)-tuple from bb, compared by the comparison applied to zz, z′z' and the order pattern of ss and s′s'. It is required that P(b)P(b) is a strict linear order for every bb, and that P(f)(w)≤P(g)(w)P(f)(w) \le P(g)(w) for all w∈P(b)w \in P(b) whenever f,g:b→b′f, g : b \to b' are embeddings with f≤gf \le g pointwise, where P(f)(z,s)=(z,f∘s)P(f)(z, s) = (z, f \circ s). The second requirement is part of Girard’s definition of a predilator (Girard 1981, Definition 4.4.1(iii)); it is verified in Lemma 3.1 so that the functors built there are predilators in his sense, and it is not used otherwise. A recursive predilator is a functor on linear orders that preserves direct limits and pullbacks, and P(n)P(n) may be infinite for finite nn. A recursive dilator is a recursive predilator DD such that D(b)D(b) is a well-order for every well-order bb. Sums and compositions of recursive predilators are recursive predilators, with indices computed primitive recursively.

3.1 The Kleene–Brouwer order of a labelled tree

A pattern of length nn consists of rows (ki,mi)(k_i, m_i), i<ni < n, with a tag ki∈ωk_i \in \omega and a number mi∈ωm_i \in \omega of slots, together with a total preorder on the set of all slots {(i,j):i<n,j<mi}\{(i, j) : i < n,\ j < m_i\}. A pattern set is a set 𝒮\mathcal S of patterns that contains the empty pattern and is closed under restriction to the first n′n' rows. For a linear order bb, 𝒮(b)\mathcal S(b) is the tree of all finite sequences ((ki,a→i))i<n((k_i, \vec a_i))_{i < n} with a→i∈bmi\vec a_i \in b^{m_i} whose pattern, given by the tags, the lengths and the order of the labels a→i\vec a_i in bb, is in 𝒮\mathcal S. Rows (k,a→)(k, \vec a) are ordered by the code of (k,m)(k, m) and then lexicographically. P𝒮(b)P_{\mathcal S}(b) is the set of nodes of 𝒮(b)\mathcal S(b) in the Kleene–Brouwer order: ss is below s′s' if ss properly extends s′s', or ss has the smaller row at the first place where they differ. The rank of a node ss is sup⁡{rk⁡(s′)+1:s′ a child of s}\sup\{\operatorname{rk}(s') + 1 : s' \text{ a child of } s\}, and ∞\infty if an infinite branch passes through it; rk⁡𝒮(b)\operatorname{rk} \mathcal S(b) is the rank of the root. Turning a tree into a functor of the order from which its labels are taken is the standard way to obtain predilators (Girard 1981).

Lemma 3.1. If 𝒮\mathcal S is recursive then P𝒮P_{\mathcal S} is a recursive predilator, with an index in Pre\mathrm{Pre} that is primitive recursive in an index of 𝒮\mathcal S. For a well-order bb, P𝒮(b)P_{\mathcal S}(b) is a well-order if and only if 𝒮(b)\mathcal S(b) has no infinite branch, and then |P𝒮(b)|≥rk⁡𝒮(b)+1|P_{\mathcal S}(b)| \ge \operatorname{rk} \mathcal S(b) + 1.

Proof.

The Kleene–Brouwer order is linear. A trace is a pattern in 𝒮\mathcal S, its arity is the number of classes of its preorder, and a node is a trace together with the increasing tuple of its distinct labels. Whether one node is below another is determined by the two patterns and the order pattern of the two tuples, recursively. An embedding ff acts by relabelling, which keeps patterns and the order of rows. If f≤gf \le g pointwise and f∘s≠g∘sf \circ s \ne g \circ s, the first row where they differ has the same tag in both and a smaller label in f∘sf \circ s at the first difference, so f∘sf \circ s is below g∘sg \circ s. A branch gives a descending sequence of its initial segments. If bb is a well-order and 𝒮(b)\mathcal S(b) has no infinite branch, the rows are well-ordered and the nodes extending a node ss form the ordered sum of the corresponding sets for the children of ss, followed by ss; by induction on the rank this is a well-order of type at least rk⁡(s)+1\operatorname{rk}(s) + 1. ◻

Lemma B.1. For every recursive pattern set 𝒮\mathcal S there is a primitive recursive pattern set 𝒮c\mathcal S^c such that 𝒮c(n)\mathcal S^c(n) is finite for finite nn, the functor n↦P𝒮c(n)n \mapsto P_{\mathcal S^c}(n) is primitive recursive on objects and maps, and rk⁡𝒮c(b)=rk⁡𝒮(b)\operatorname{rk} \mathcal S^c(b) = \operatorname{rk} \mathcal S(b) for every infinite well-order bb.

The tree 𝒮(n)\mathcal S(n) can be infinite for finite nn, because the depth of nodes and the data in a row can be unbounded; a row without slots that may be repeated gives infinite depth already at n=0n = 0. Also, membership in 𝒮\mathcal S is only recursive. In 𝒮c\mathcal S^c each row therefore carries a computation that verifies membership, which makes the test primitive recursive, and a block of extra slots, at least as many as the size of what the row contains, whose labels must all be different. Proof.

A row of a pattern in 𝒮c\mathcal S^c is a row of a pattern in 𝒮\mathcal S whose tag is extended by a halting computation showing that the pattern up to this row is in 𝒮\mathcal S, followed by a block of extra slots whose number exceeds the code of the extended tag and the number of original slots. It is required that the labels in the extra slots of a node strictly increase in their order of occurrence through the whole node; the order between extra labels and original labels is not restricted. On a finite order nn a node has at most nn extra labels, so the number of its rows and the codes of its tags are bounded by nn, each row has at most 2n2n slots, and membership is a bounded test.
